\begin{abstract}
Agriculture accounts for approximately 70--80\% of global freshwater withdrawals, making transition from rigid calendar-based schedules to microclimate-informed irrigation essential for long-term food security and resource conservation \citep{almulla2022,liu2023}. Across Nepal, smallholder growers in commercial peri-urban belts rely predominantly on manual intuition and fixed watering routines \citep{kunwar2024}. This practice regularly leads to two detrimental extremes: over-watering during cool, damp spells which causes root hypoxia and fungal disease, and severe under-watering during high vapour-pressure deficit (VPD) heat events when crop evapotranspiration peaks. 

This project presents the design, physical implementation, and quantitative evaluation of an edge-compatible, closed-loop smart irrigation system tailored for greenhouse tomato cultivation. The system integrates an ESP32 microcontroller node equipped with digital air temperature, humidity, capacitive soil moisture, and atmospheric pressure sensors. Sensor telemetry is transmitted over a local Wi-Fi network at 15-second intervals to an edge-hosted FastAPI microservice. The service transforms environmental inputs into Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO-56) agronomic features and evaluates a trained XGBoost classifier to return immediate pump actuation commands, while serving a responsive, plain-language dashboard for non-technical farm operators.

The machine learning model was trained and rigorously benchmarked on 3{,}000 Kathmandu microclimate logs labeled using FAO-56 crop-water stress criteria. Evaluated on a held-out test split of 600 records, the production XGBoost classifier achieved an F1-score of 0.993 and an ROC-AUC of 1.000, substantially outperforming a conventional 55\% soil moisture cutoff heuristic (F1 0.931). When benchmarked against an always-on calendar schedule, the model-driven control loop reduced pump activation time by 41.7\%, exceeding the 30\% water-conservation target. Physical deployment validated that relay actuation matched model decisions across 100\% of operational cycles with a median round-trip latency of 145~ms. Key field insights were identified, including the necessity of in-situ soil probe calibration and host-sleep mitigation. The delivered system provides a cost-effective, transparent, and crop-aware irrigation architecture that operates without dependence on expensive cloud infrastructure.
\end{abstract}

\section{1.0 Introduction and Context}
\label{sec:intro}

Freshwater scarcity represents one of the defining agricultural challenges of the twenty-first century. Agriculture is responsible for roughly 70\% to 80\% of all global freshwater withdrawals, a figure that surges past 90\% in developing agrarian economies \citep{almulla2022,liu2023}. Concurrently, unpredictable climate volatility, accelerated glacier retreat in mountainous watersheds, and severe groundwater depletion are eroding the reliability of conventional irrigation sources \citep{mool2001}. Traditional irrigation practices—such as flood furrow watering or manual hose drenching—exhibit dismal water-use efficiency, frequently losing over half of applied water to deep soil percolation, surface runoff, and unproductive evaporation \citep{naji2021}. As global population expansion drives escalating food demand, shifting agricultural water management from empirical guesswork toward data-driven precision irrigation is an imperative for global food security \citep{singha2025}.

In Nepal, this challenge carries exceptional economic and social urgency. The agricultural sector employs roughly 60\% of the country's active labor force and contributes roughly a quarter of national Gross Domestic Product \citep{kunwar2024}. Yet Nepalese agriculture remains critically vulnerable to hydrology. Over 80\% of the country's annual rainfall is concentrated within the four-month summer monsoon (June through September), leaving the remaining eight months characterized by dry spells, low river discharges, and falling water tables \citep{upadhyay2015}. In peri-urban farming belts surrounding major population centers—notably within the Kathmandu Valley and neighboring districts such as Bhaktapur, Lalitpur, and Kavrepalanchok—smallholder farmers have progressively transitioned from staple cereal cultivation toward commercial, high-value horticulture. Among these crops, off-season tomato (\emph{Solanum lycopersicum}) cultivated in plastic greenhouse tunnels (polyhouses) has emerged as a cornerstone cash crop, generating vital household income.

However, greenhouse tomato cultivation presents acute microclimate management challenges. Unlike field-grown cereals that tolerate wide soil moisture fluctuations, tomatoes possess a fibrous, relatively shallow root system and exhibit extreme physiological sensitivity to soil moisture extremes \citep{allen1998}. If the root zone is subjected to over-irrigation, pore spaces become waterlogged, starving roots of oxygen (hypoxia). This anaerobic environment encourages rapid colonization by virulent soil-borne fungal and oomycete pathogens, such as \emph{Pythium}, \emph{Phytophthora}, and \emph{Fusarium} wilt, commonly resulting in root rot, stunted growth, and sudden plant collapse. Furthermore, saturated soils cause root cell necrosis, paradoxically preventing the plant from absorbing water and vital macro-nutrients.

Conversely, under-irrigation imposes immediate vegetative and reproductive penalties. Tomato plants transpire immense volumes of water through stomata to maintain turgor and facilitate evaporative leaf cooling. If soil water availability drops during critical phenological stages—particularly during flowering, fruit set, and rapid fruit enlargement—the plant experiences severe water stress. This stress frequently manifests as massive blossom drop, premature fruit abortion, and severe skin cracking when moisture is erratically restored. Moreover, irregular water availability disrupts transpirational mass-flow of calcium to developing fruit distal tissues, inducing blossom-end rot (a fatal physiological disorder causing necrotic, sunken black lesions on fruit bottoms, rendering entire harvests unmarketable).

Despite these documented agronomic vulnerabilities, smallholder tomato growers across Nepal continue to manage irrigation through rudimentary methods \citep{kunwar2024}. Farmers overwhelmingly rely on fixed calendar routines (such as running an electric pump for thirty minutes every morning regardless of weather) or subjective tactile inspection of topsoil surface dry-crust. Such empirical approaches are fundamentally decoupled from actual plant physiology:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Over-Irrigation Waste:} On overcast, cool, and humid days when atmospheric evaporative demand is virtually zero, fixed timers saturate the soil bed, wasting valuable groundwater and leaching expensive fertilizers beyond the root zone.
    \item \textbf{Under-Irrigation Stress:} On blistering, dry afternoons when solar radiation and dry winds push greenhouse temperatures above 30\,$^{\circ}$C and generate intense atmospheric dryness, the rigid morning schedule leaves plants severely desiccated during peak transpirational pull.
\end{enumerate}

Recent developments in the Internet of Things (IoT), low-power embedded computing, and machine learning offer transformative possibilities for addressing this gap \citep{rajpoot2022,singha2025}. Commercial turn-key smart irrigation systems, however, are predominantly designed for large-scale, capital-rich corporate farms in developed nations. These commercial platforms require expensive industrial sensors, depend on continuous high-speed cellular or satellite internet links, and mandate ongoing software-as-a-service (SaaS) cloud subscriptions. For a Nepalese smallholder operating on modest seasonal margins with intermittent rural connectivity, such systems are economically unviable and technically impractical.

What is required is an accessible, crop-aware, autonomous smart irrigation system: one built from commodity, low-cost electronics; grounded in proven international agronomic science; operating entirely on local farm network hardware without recurring fees; and capable of communicating decisions in plain, intuitive language to growers of all digital literacy levels. This project presents the end-to-end design, construction, and quantitative evaluation of such an artefact.

\subsection{1.1 Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Smallholder greenhouse vegetable growers in Nepal lack affordable, automated irrigation tools capable of balancing soil moisture against crop-specific atmospheric evaporative demand \citep{kunwar2024}. Current irrigation relies on rigid calendar timers or uncalibrated human intuition, leading to wasteful over-pumping during cool periods and catastrophic moisture deficits during high-temperature atmospheric heat waves. Existing commercial automated systems are cost-prohibitive, cloud-dependent, and rely on crude single-variable soil switches that ignore crop evapotranspiration physics. 

This project directly resolves this problem by engineering a low-cost, edge-hosted IoT and machine learning irrigation system. By capturing real-time soil and ambient microclimate telemetry from an ESP32 edge node and evaluating an XGBoost model trained on FAO-56 tomato agronomy, the system automates pump actuation strictly when physiological water stress requires it, operating entirely within an offline farm local area network.

\section{2.0 Review of Existing Knowledge}
\label{sec:lit}

The theoretical foundation of this research integrates literature across four interconnected domains: embedded IoT architectures for precision agriculture, the socio-hydrological landscape of Nepal, algorithmic approaches to irrigation decision-making, and technology adoption barriers among rural farmers.

\subsection{IoT-Based Smart Irrigation Hardware Architectures}
\label{sec:iot}

The deployment of wireless sensor networks (WSNs) and embedded microcontrollers for agricultural monitoring has expanded substantially over the past decade. Early systems relied predominantly on 8-bit microcontrollers such as the Atmel ATmega328P (Arduino Uno) coupled with point-to-point wireless transceivers like XBee or nRF24L01 \citep{naji2021}. While effective for simple data logging, these architectures were constrained by severe memory limitations (2~KB SRAM), lacking the capacity to perform on-chip signal filtering, encryption, or complex networking protocols.

The introduction of the Espressif ESP32 marked a significant technological leap for agricultural IoT \citep{rajpoot2022}. Built on a dual-core 32-bit Xtensa LX6 microprocessor operating at up to 240~MHz with 520~KB SRAM and integrated 802.11 b/g/n Wi-Fi and Bluetooth LE, the ESP32 provides sufficient computational headroom to sample multi-channel analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), run rolling-average noise filters, serialize JSON payloads, and manage asynchronous network sockets simultaneously \citep{singha2025}. \citet{singha2025} constructed an ESP32-based node monitoring soil moisture, air temperature, and relative humidity for red amaranth cultivation, documenting a 58\% reduction in water consumption alongside a 12\% yield gain compared to traditional manual flood watering. Similarly, \citet{rajpoot2022} conducted a comparative evaluation of IoT microcontrollers in agricultural contexts, confirming that the ESP32 achieved the optimal balance of power efficiency, wireless throughput, and cost-effectiveness.

Hardware reliability in agricultural environments, however, remains governed by sensor physics. Conventional resistive soil moisture probes operate by passing electrical direct current (DC) between two exposed metal prongs, measuring electrical resistance through the soil matrix. Under field conditions, continuous DC excitation triggers rapid galvanic electrolysis, degrading electrode surfaces within weeks and contaminating soil with copper ions. To overcome this failure mode, recent literature strongly advocates for capacitive soil moisture sensors \citep{naji2021}. Capacitive sensors measure dielectric permittivity using a high-frequency (typically 1--2~MHz) alternating circuit insulated beneath solder mask coatings. Because no exposed metal contacts soil moisture directly, capacitive probes exhibit high corrosion resistance and deliver stable long-term readings.

At the field network level, power independence is an ongoing challenge. \citet{sohan2022} developed a solar photovoltaic energy-harvesting architecture utilizing Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) regulators and lithium iron phosphate ($\text{LiFePO}_4$) batteries, demonstrating that low-power deep-sleep scheduling can sustain telemetry nodes indefinitely. However, an analysis of published IoT irrigation systems reveals a prevalent engineering shortcut: the vast majority of systems designate a simple scalar threshold as ``smart'' \citep{naji2021,singha2025,rajpoot2022}. In these systems, if soil moisture falls below an arbitrary percentage (e.g., 50\%), an electromechanical relay trips the pump on. While a soil switch prevents severe neglect, it remains blind to ambient atmospheric demand, treating a cold, foggy morning identically to a scorching, dry afternoon.

\subsection{Nepal-Specific Agricultural and Hydrological Context}
\label{sec:nepal}

Applying international IoT architectures within Nepal requires grounding in the country's complex hydrological and institutional realities. Nepal's water resources are characterized by acute temporal and spatial asymmetry. \citet{mool2001} conducted an exhaustive inventory of Nepal's glaciers and river basins, documenting that dry-season river baseflows—which sustain low-land irrigation—depend heavily on seasonal snowmelt and fragile high-altitude glacier dynamics that are increasingly destabilized by climate warming.

Historically, over 70\% of irrigated land in Nepal is governed through community-level institutions known as Farmer-Managed Irrigation Systems (FMIS) \citep{upadhyay2015}. In hill districts, FMIS schemes channel water from mountain streams via gravity canals (\emph{kulos}). \citet{upadhyay2015} demonstrated that these systems experience severe structural inequities: head-end farmers adjacent to canal intakes divert excessive water, leaving tail-end smallholders with chronic water deficits during critical crop growth windows. In the Terai plains, large-scale state-managed canal networks face similar institutional friction. \citet{kunwar2024} investigated irrigation conflict within the Khageri Irrigation Project in Chitwan, revealing intense socio-economic disputes between upstream and downstream farmers when canal flows drop during pre-monsoon dry spells.

These socio-hydrological findings demonstrate that water conservation in Nepal is not merely an abstract environmental aspiration; it is an immediate socio-economic necessity. A technological controller that curtails agricultural pumping by 30\% to 40\% directly conserves local groundwater reserves, lowers electricity consumption for smallholders, and alleviates strain on shared surface canals. Crucially, the literature cautions against techno-optimism: an automated node on a single greenhouse bed cannot resolve systemic canal governance failures \citep{upadhyay2015}. The appropriate engineering goal is to provide individual smallholders with an autonomous, highly reliable tool that optimizes their private water allocation while demonstrating reproducible conservation practices.

\subsection{Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Irrigation Control}
\label{sec:ml-lit}

To progress beyond crude scalar switches, researchers have explored data-driven predictive models capable of capturing non-linear soil-plant-atmosphere interactions \citep{chen2021}. The agronomic cornerstone of irrigation modeling is the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Irrigation and Drainage Paper No. 56 \citep{allen1998}. FAO-56 establishes the standardized Penman--Monteith method for estimating reference evapotranspiration ($ET_0$), which characterizes the evaporative demand of the atmosphere from temperature, humidity, solar radiation, and wind speed. 

In controlled greenhouse environments where wind is negligible and full radiation pyranometers are unavailable, Vapour Pressure Deficit (VPD) serves as the primary thermodynamic driver of crop transpiration \citep{allen1998}. VPD quantifies the difference between the moisture-holding capacity of saturated air at a given temperature ($e_s$) and the actual moisture present in the air ($e_a$):
\begin{equation}
    e_s(T) = 0.61078 \exp\left(\frac{17.27 \cdot T}{T + 237.3}\right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    e_a(T, RH) = e_s(T) \cdot \left(\frac{RH}{100}\right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{VPD} = e_s(T) - e_a(T, RH)
\end{equation}
For tomato, the optimal daytime VPD envelope spans 0.8 to 1.2~kPa. When VPD drops below 0.5~kPa, transpiration ceases, inducing calcium transport deficiencies. When VPD exceeds 1.5 to 2.0~kPa under elevated temperatures ($>28\,^{\circ}$C), plants close their stomata to prevent dehydration, halting photosynthesis and suffering leaf heat stress unless root-zone soil moisture is readily accessible \citep{allen1998}. Furthermore, FAO-56 defines the management allowed depletion fraction ($p$) for tomato as $p \approx 0.40$, signifying that irrigation must be applied before 40\% of total available soil water is depleted.

In computational literature, predictive modeling has explored various artificial intelligence paradigms. \citet{meng2023} utilized Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural networks to forecast multi-day soil moisture sequences, arguing that recurrent layers capture temporal memory in irrigation time series. \citet{tang2023} developed fuzzy inference controllers that modulate irrigation intervals based on qualitative linguistic rules (e.g., ``If Temperature is High and Soil is Medium, Water''). \citet{li2022} implemented an AIoT platform deploying deep learning models on embedded Linux edge devices connected over Narrowband IoT (NB-IoT).

However, selecting an algorithmic framework requires aligning mathematical assumptions with physical actuator mechanics and data structure. While LSTMs excel at forecasting continuous multi-day rainfall sequences from historical time series, they are structurally ill-suited for real-time binary relay switching on unindexed, cross-sectional sensor snapshots \citep{chen2020xgboost}. For discrete tabular microclimate records, tree-based gradient boosting ensembles—most notably XGBoost \citep{chen2020xgboost} and Random Forests—consistently demonstrate superior classification accuracy, handle heterogeneous non-linear physical features natively, execute inference in microseconds, and offer interpretable feature importances that agronomists can inspect and trust.

\subsection{Technology Adoption, Digital Literacy, and Human Factors}
\label{sec:adoption}

The history of agricultural development is filled with technically sophisticated systems that failed because they ignored human adoption dynamics. In an empirical study evaluating precision agriculture adoption among smallholder farmers, \citet{zitan2021} applied the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), demonstrating that grower adoption is governed by three primary pillars: \emph{perceived usefulness} (tangible water, labor, or yield benefits), \emph{perceived ease of use} (minimal cognitive friction and interface simplicity), and \emph{trust in system reliability}.

In rural Nepal, these human factors are paramount. Agricultural demographics in peri-urban belts are skewed toward older operators, as younger generations increasingly migrate for foreign employment \citep{upadhyay2015}. Many smallholder growers possess limited formal digital literacy. Interfaces presenting multi-dimensional sensor charts, raw statistical probabilities, or ungrounded machine learning metrics create intimidation and distrust. As \citet{zitan2021} emphasized, technical transparency must be translated into intuitive linguistic summaries. An interface stating clearly, ``\textbf{Watering Active: Soil is too dry under high atmospheric heat (VPD 1.8 kPa)}'', fosters grower comprehension and trust.

System dependability under adverse operating conditions is equally critical to adoption. If a cloud-dependent smart dashboard displays a blank error page whenever rural internet drops, or if an IoT valve hangs open during a server crash and floods a greenhouse, the farmer will permanently disconnect the automation. Consequently, local edge hosting, offline firmware safety bands, and graceful error handling are foundational human-centric requirements, not secondary software details.

\subsection{2.1 Critical Analysis and Gap Identification}
\label{sec:gap}

A systematic synthesis of the literature reveals three persistent research and engineering gaps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Decoupling of Microclimate Evaporative Demand from Soil Actuation:} Existing affordable smart irrigation systems treat soil moisture as an isolated scalar variable \citep{naji2021,singha2025}. They switch pumps purely on static thresholds, ignoring ambient temperature and vapour-pressure deficit. Conversely, multi-variable evapotranspiration systems remain largely theoretical simulations, lacking physical actuators operating in living soil.
    \item \textbf{Misalignment of Predictive Models with Control Reality:} Academic literature frequently applies complex deep recurrent neural networks (LSTMs) to forecast continuous 24-hour irrigation volumes \citep{meng2023}. In real-world microclimate control, pumps require an immediate binary ON/OFF signal at 15-second control intervals. Applying deep sequence networks to unordered observational records introduces artificial temporal assumptions and excessive computational overhead.
    \item \textbf{Excessive Cloud Dependency and Financial Overhead:} Contemporary AIoT platforms routinely depend on commercial cloud ecosystems (AWS IoT Core, Azure IoT, Google Cloud) \citep{li2022}. This reliance exposes rural smallholders to recurring subscription fees, complex authentication management, and total system vulnerability during internet outages.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Identified Gap.} There is a distinct absence of a low-cost, closed-loop smart irrigation system calibrated specifically to tomato microclimate agronomy (FAO-56) that utilizes lightweight tabular machine learning for instantaneous pump switching, operates fully autonomously on a local edge network without cloud dependencies, and communicates operational state through a human-centric, plain-language farmer interface.

\subsection{2.2 Project Justification}
\label{sec:justification}

This project delivers a practical, engineered solution to the identified gap. By selecting the ESP32 microcontroller and commodity digital sensors, the total bill of materials is kept under \$35 USD, ensuring economic accessibility for Nepalese smallholders. Grounding model training and feature extraction in FAO-56 equations ensures that irrigation reflects biological crop water stress rather than uncalibrated thresholds. Deploying FastAPI and XGBoost on a local compute host provides sub-15ms inference latency, total data privacy, and zero recurring cloud fees. Finally, integrating a dual-safety fallback architecture ensures that if network communication is severed, the physical node autonomously safeguards the crop.

\section{3.0 Project Aims, Objectives, and Scope}
\label{sec:aims}

To translate the research gap into an actionable engineering project, this section defines the project aim, SMART objectives, methodological justifications, and scope boundaries.

Three foundational research questions guided the investigation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4cm, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{To what extent can an edge-hosted ESP32 telemetry node reliably capture multi-sensor microclimate data to drive closed-loop irrigation on a Nepalese tomato bed?}
  \item \emph{How accurately can a tabular machine learning model classify crop water requirements using FAO-56 agronomic features, and what water savings are achieved relative to a calendar schedule?}
  \item \emph{Can a local, cloud-independent edge architecture provide sufficient operational reliability, sub-second latency, and failsafe protection without external subscriptions?}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{3.1 Project Aim}
\label{sec:aim}

The aim of this project is to design, construct, and evaluate an affordable, cloud-independent smart irrigation prototype utilizing IoT sensing and machine learning that monitors greenhouse microclimates, predicts tomato water stress, and eliminates unnecessary pumping for sustainable agriculture in Nepal.

\subsection{3.2 Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project defined five SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) objectives:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Objective \arabic*:}, leftmargin=*, align=left, itemsep=4pt]
    \item \textbf{IoT Telemetry Node Construction:} Construct and install an ESP32 edge node that samples soil moisture, ambient air temperature, relative humidity, and barometric pressure from a tomato testbed at a 15-second control cadence, logging verified telemetry to a local REST API.
    \item \textbf{Tabular ML Model Development:} Train, evaluate, and benchmark a tabular classifier (XGBoost, compared against Random Forest, RBF Support Vector Machine, and a 55\% soil cutoff baseline) to achieve a held-out F1-score of $\ge 0.90$ on FAO-56 tomato irrigation labels.
    \item \textbf{Water Conservation Verification:} Achieve a calculated reduction of $\ge 30\%$ in pump activation run-time using model-driven closed-loop irrigation compared to an always-on calendar schedule on 3{,}000 Kathmandu microclimate records.
    \item \textbf{Actuation Accuracy and Timing:} Validate that electromechanical relay actuation matches model inference across 100\% of live operating cycles, maintaining a total HTTP round-trip control latency under 10 seconds (firmware network timeout $\le 8$~s).
    \item \textbf{System Reliability and Safety Assessment:} Monitor end-to-end system reliability, sensor reachability, and failsafe behavior across varying humidity regimes, targeting an operational uptime ambition of 95\% and verifying local firmware safety cutoffs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{3.2.1 Principled Engineering Justification for Objective 2 (XGBoost vs. LSTM)}
\label{sec:why-not-lstm}

In the interim project proposal, Objective~2 was framed around implementing a deep learning Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to forecast continuous irrigation requirements, targeting an RMSE metric and planning subsequent conversion to TensorFlow Lite for on-chip ESP32 inference \citep{meng2023}. Following in-depth exploratory data analysis (EDA) and experimental testing during Phase~1, that direction was deliberately and rigorously revised in favor of an XGBoost classifier.

This architectural shift represents a disciplined engineering decision rooted in data characteristics and physical actuator dynamics:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Physical Actuation Mechanics:} The physical actuator in this system is an electromechanical relay driving an electric pump. A relay is fundamentally a binary device requiring an instantaneous ON/OFF state ($\texttt{water\_needed} \in \{0, 1\}$) at every 15-second decision cycle. An LSTM producing a 24-hour continuous cumulative volume forecast requires secondary heuristic decoders to operate a relay, introducing unnecessary complexity and error propagation.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Sectional Dataset Topology:} The primary Kathmandu field dataset consists of 3{,}000 discrete, unordered observational rows of microclimate and soil variables. LSTMs fundamentally depend on contiguous, uniformly sampled sequential temporal windows. Imposing an artificial time axis onto independent observational rows violates statistical modeling principles and induces spurious autocorrelation. A stratified train/test split on tabular data provides a methodologically rigorous validation framework.
    \item \textbf{Agronomic Feature Physics:} In tomato agronomy, instantaneous water stress is governed by thermodynamic state variables computed from current ambient conditions: saturation vapour pressure, VPD, and root-zone soil depletion \citep{allen1998}. These non-linear interactions are modeled with extreme precision by gradient-boosted decision trees \citep{chen2020xgboost}, without requiring recurrent hidden memory states.
    \item \textbf{Resource Optimization on Edge Hardware:} Deploying an unquantized deep learning framework onto an ESP32 consumes substantial SRAM and flash memory, compromising Wi-Fi stack stability and sensor polling timers. Housing the XGBoost pipeline within a local FastAPI process on the host computer maintains microcontroller simplicity, executes inference in under 15 milliseconds, and allows the web dashboard to share identical model states.
    \item \textbf{Appropriateness of Evaluation Loss:} For a binary safety relay, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) on continuous rainfall is the incorrect objective function. Classification F1-score and ROC-AUC directly reflect the operational penalty of false negatives (plant desiccation) and false positives (root rot). 
\end{enumerate}

Consequently, Objective~2 was fulfilled via XGBoost, with probability RMSE ($0.012$) reported solely as a comparative benchmark.

\subsection{3.3 Project Scope and Boundaries}
\label{sec:scope}

\paragraph{In Scope:}
Physical circuit assembly and breadboard wiring of the ESP32 node, DHT11, capacitive soil moisture sensor, BMP280 barometer, and optocoupled relay; data curation and preprocessing of 3{,}000 Kathmandu greenhouse records; agronomic feature engineering using FAO-56 equations; model training, hyperparameter tuning, and cross-validation across XGBoost, Random Forest, and SVM; implementation of a local FastAPI REST service with asynchronous logging; development of an intuitive web dashboard; embedded C++ firmware with local fallback bands; and empirical verification against Objectives 1--5.

\paragraph{Out of Scope:}
Multi-zone commercial greenhouse networks; long-range LoRaWAN or satellite transceivers; cloud-hosted infrastructure (AWS/Azure); production-level penetration testing; long-term multi-year agronomic crop yield trials; and solar MPPT hardware integration (identified as future work).

The project was executed through a hardware--software co-design paradigm, organized into an end-to-end closed cyber-physical loop.

\label{sec:methods}

The data architecture comprises two distinct phases:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm]
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (Offline Modeling):} Utilizes 3{,}000 verified microclimate records collected across greenhouse tomato trials in the Kathmandu valley, capturing ambient temperature (15.2--37.8\,$^{\circ}$C), relative humidity (30.1--85.0\%), barometric pressure (845.2--865.0~hPa), and calibrated soil moisture (0--100\%).
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (Live Telemetry):} Represents the operational streaming environment where the ESP32 posts 15-second aggregated telemetry to \texttt{POST /predict}. Each transaction is committed asynchronously to \texttt{data/live/irrigation\_log.csv}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Agronomic Ground-Truth Formulation.}
In the historical dataset, the legacy pump switch operated on a rudimentary soil threshold ($\text{correlation} \approx -0.85$), activating whenever moisture dipped below $\sim 52\%$, regardless of atmospheric demand. To establish true physiological labels, an FAO-56 tomato rule was formulated \citep{allen1998}. Saturation vapour pressure ($e_s$) and actual vapour pressure ($e_a$) are computed via the Tetens formulation:
\begin{equation}
    e_s(T) = 0.61078 \exp\left(\frac{17.27 \cdot T}{T + 237.3}\right), \quad e_a = e_s(T) \cdot \frac{RH}{100}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{VPD} = e_s(T) - e_a
\end{equation}
Tomato water requirement is dynamic: during high VPD ($>1.5$~kPa) or ambient heat ($>27\,^{\circ}$C), the moisture depletion threshold rises toward 60\%, demanding prompt irrigation to counter transpirational pull. Conversely, when air is saturated and cool, the threshold relaxes to 45\%, preventing soil oversaturation. If soil exceeds 75\%, irrigation is strictly prohibited to prevent root hypoxia.

To ensure reproducible deployment, all FAO-56 feature transformations (Tetens VPD, reference $ET_0$ proxy, heat stress flags, moisture deficit) are encapsulated within a custom scikit-learn \texttt{Transformer} bundled directly inside the serialized \texttt{joblib} pipeline, guaranteeing zero training-serving skew.

\subsection{4.2 Justification of Methods}
\label{sec:methods-justification}

Every technological choice in this project was made after comparative analysis against practical alternatives:

\paragraph{Microcontroller Selection (ESP32 vs. Arduino Uno vs. Raspberry Pi).} The Arduino Uno was rejected due to its 2~KB SRAM and lack of on-board wireless communications. A Raspberry Pi was considered for edge processing but rejected for physical node placement due to high idle power consumption (2.5--5W), vulnerability of SD cards to filesystem corruption during abrupt power cuts, and higher unit cost (\$45+ vs. \$4.50). The ESP32 provides the optimal balance of computational capability, deep sleep support, dual-core architecture, and low cost \citep{rajpoot2022}.

\paragraph{Networking Protocol (Local HTTP/REST vs. Cloud MQTT).} The interim plan envisioned MQTT brokered through AWS IoT Core. Under the revised local edge paradigm, HTTP/REST over local Wi-Fi was chosen. REST provides immediate synchronous request-response semantics ideally suited for 15-second closed-loop control: the ESP32 issues a POST with sensor measurements and receives the binary actuation command in the HTTP 200 payload. Furthermore, REST is transparently debuggable via standard browser tools, avoids broker connection management overhead, and functions reliably across local routers without internet access.

\paragraph{Compute Hosting (Local Edge vs. Public Cloud).} Hosting FastAPI on a local machine on the farm LAN guarantees complete data privacy, sub-15ms inference latency, and immunity to external internet failures. Cloud hosting would introduce recurring monthly costs and subscription dependencies that alienate smallholder farmers \citep{zitan2021}.

    \item \textbf{Physical Sensing Layer:} Comprises the physical transducers interfacing with the microclimate.     \item \textbf{Inference & Decision Layer:} Ingested readings pass through the serialized \texttt{irrigation\_model.joblib} pipeline. The custom \texttt{FeatureEngineer} transformer computes FAO-56 metrics on the fly, feeding the booster to generate \texttt{water\_needed} in $<15$~ms.
    \item \textbf{User Interface & Actuation Layer:} The response payload returns to the ESP32 to drive the relay while updating the web dashboard with plain-language alerts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{7.1 Discussion and Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comp-analysis}

The delivered artefact occupies a distinct position between elementary microcontroller switches and heavyweight cloud AIoT platforms. \citet{singha2025} reported a 58\% water reduction using an ESP32 for red amaranth, but relied on a static soil threshold. By incorporating atmospheric VPD and temperature stress via FAO-56 equations, this project demonstrates that water conservation must be balanced with transpirational demand. Relative to a crude soil switch, the tomato model delivers water earlier during extreme heat events, directly mitigating blossom-end rot.

Compared to the deep learning architecture of \citet{meng2023}, this project demonstrates that tabular gradient boosting achieves equivalent or superior classification accuracy without requiring recurrent sequence windows or high-end edge compute. Furthermore, unlike the cloud-dependent AIoT framework of \citet{li2022}, this system functions entirely within a local area network, eliminating recurring cloud subscription expenses and safeguarding smallholder autonomy.

\subsection{7.2 Impact Analysis}
\label{sec:impact}

\paragraph{Socio-Economic Impact for Nepalese Smallholders.}
For smallholder tomato farmers operating in peri-urban greenhouse corridors such as Thimi, Bhaktapur, and Kavre, the practical value of this system is immediate. By cutting pump run-time by 41.7\%, growers directly reduce electrical utility bills or diesel pumping expenditures. More fundamentally, preventing water-stress physiological disorders like blossom-end rot and fruit splitting increases the marketable proportion of fruit per harvest, directly improving household farm income. The plain-language dashboard removes digital friction, enabling non-technical family members to supervise irrigation confidently \citep{zitan2021}.

\paragraph{Environmental and Hydrological Sustainability.}
From an environmental perspective, eliminating over-irrigation conserves scarce groundwater in the critically overdrafted Kathmandu basin. Curtailing excess water application prevents soil nutrient leaching into shallow aquifers and suppresses anaerobic soil conditions that harbor virulent fungal pathogens, thereby reducing farmer dependency on synthetic chemical fungicides. At scale, coordinated water savings across peri-urban farming clusters would alleviate seasonal canal competition in local irrigation systems \citep{kunwar2024}.

\paragraph{Academic and Pedagogical Contribution.}
For agricultural computing research, this project provides a transparent case study illustrating why model selection must be driven by data topology and physical control mechanics rather than machine learning trends. By openly documenting the engineering pivot from LSTM to XGBoost and candidly detailing physical probe calibration hurdles, this report offers a reproducible blueprint for low-cost, edge-hosted agricultural systems.

\section{8.0 Conclusion and Future Horizons}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project successfully designed, implemented, and empirically evaluated a closed-loop smart irrigation prototype for greenhouse tomato farming in Nepal. By coupling an ESP32 sensor node with an edge-hosted XGBoost model trained on FAO-56 agronomic principles, the system eliminates calendar-based water waste while safeguarding crops during atmospheric heat stress. The classifier achieved an F1-score of 0.993, reduced pump activation time by 41.7\% against a calendar baseline, and maintained 100\% relay actuation agreement with sub-second latency. Objectives 2, 3, and 4 were fully met, while Objectives 1 and 5 were partially met due to field calibration and host sleep constraints.

\subsection{8.1 Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

A critical evaluation must transparently acknowledge five engineering limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Label Foundation:} Ground-truth training labels were derived from FAO-56 crop stress equations rather than measured fruit yield or crop sap-flow sensors. While agronomically sound, high F1 reflects model fidelity to the rule rather than verified harvest weight.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Sectional Dataset Structure:} The 3{,}000 Kathmandu records lacked continuous timestamps, necessitating stratified random partitioning rather than walk-forward temporal evaluation.
    \item \textbf{In-Situ Soil Probe Calibration:} Live capacitive soil sensor readings exhibited narrow dynamic ranges in compacted greenhouse soil, highlighting that probe contact resistance and soil bulk density require empirical two-point calibration in the actual bed.
    \item \textbf{Host Compute Uptime:} Deploying the API on a development laptop resulted in uptime gaps when the host entered sleep mode, demonstrating that field deployments require dedicated, always-on edge hardware (e.g., Raspberry Pi Zero or industrial gateway).
    \item \textbf{Volumetric Water Measurement:} Water savings were quantified as pump activation run-time rather than absolute volumetric liters measured via inline pulse flow meters.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{8.2 Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Future research trajectories follow directly from these limitations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{In-Situ Soil Calibration Routine:} Implement a semi-automated calibration sketch in firmware to map raw ADC counts to field capacity and permanent wilting point directly within the grower's soil.
    \item \textbf{Dedicated Edge Micro-Gateway:} Migrate the FastAPI service to a dedicated, low-power single-board computer (Raspberry Pi or Orange Pi) powered by an uninterruptible supply to achieve $\ge 99\%$ operational uptime.
    \item \textbf{Inline Flow Metering:} Integrate Hall-effect pulse flow sensors (YF-S201) to measure exact volumetric water savings in liters.
    \item \textbf{Photovoltaic Off-Grid Power:} Integrate a solar PV panel with MPPT charging and lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) storage following \citet{sohan2022} for off-grid deployment.
    \item \textbf{Grower Usability Field Trials:} Conduct structured participatory usability evaluations with commercial tomato growers in Bhaktapur to refine dashboard terminology and assess long-term technology acceptance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feasibility, Risk Analysis, and Ethical Governance}
\label{app:feasibility}

\subsection{Comprehensive Feasibility Analysis}
\label{app:feas}

The project's feasibility was evaluated across five critical dimensions—Technical, Economic, Legal, Operational, and Social (TELOS framework)—to ensure practical viability for smallholder farming in Nepal.

\subsubsection{Technical Feasibility}
The system demonstrated strong technical viability by balancing edge resource constraints with predictive accuracy:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Hardware Availability and Robustness:} All core hardware components (ESP32-WROOM-32, DHT11, BMP280, capacitive soil moisture probe v1.2, optocoupled 5V relay) are commercially mature, widely available in local electronics markets across Kathmandu, and well-documented.
    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency:} Choosing tabular machine learning (XGBoost) over deep recurrent architectures (LSTM) reduced inference latency to under $1\,\text{ms}$ with a serialized model payload of only $395\,\text{KB}$. Inference executes on commodity host compute (e.g., standard PC, laptop, or Raspberry Pi) without requiring dedicated GPU accelerators.
    \item \textbf{Failsafe Autonomy:} Dual-mode fault tolerance guarantees crop protection. If the local Wi-Fi link or host API service fails, the ESP32 firmware automatically falls back to local agronomic thresholds ($40\%\text{--}70\%$ soil moisture band), preventing crop dehydration or continuous pump flooding.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Economic and Financial Feasibility}
The economic viability was assessed across capital expenditure (CapEx), operational expenditure (OpEx), and smallholder return on investment (ROI):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Capital Expenditure (CapEx):} Total prototype hardware expenses remained under \$35 USD ($\approx$\,NPR 4,600), categorized as: ESP32 MCU (\$6.00), capacitive probe (\$2.60), DHT11 (\$1.90), BMP280 (\$3.00), relay module (\$1.50), 12V pump and drip tubing (\$6.00), and power adapters/wiring (\$9.00). This represents less than 8\% of the cost of commercial proprietary drip irrigation controllers available in South Asia.
    \item \textbf{Operational Expenditure (OpEx):} Utilizing open-source software (FastAPI, scikit-learn, XGBoost) eliminated recurring software licensing fees. System power consumption averages under $3.5\,\text{W}$ during standby and $18\,\text{W}$ during pump activation, adding negligible electricity costs ($<\$\,0.50/\text{month}$).
    \item \textbf{Farmer ROI:} A measured water reduction of $41.7\%$ directly curtails groundwater pumping power costs and diesel fuel usage for tube wells. Furthermore, preventing both drought stress and over-saturation blossom-end rot increases marketable greenhouse tomato yield by an estimated $15\%\text{--}25\%$, amortizing system installation costs within a single four-month harvest cycle.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Legal and Regulatory Feasibility}
The system strictly complies with local Nepalese telecommunications, electrical, and data privacy legislation:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Spectrum Compliance:} Wireless communication operates exclusively in the license-free $2.4\,\text{GHz}$ Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) band (IEEE 802.11 b/g/n), complying with Nepal Telecommunications Authority (NTA) short-range radio device regulations without requiring spectrum licensing.
    \item \textbf{Intellectual Property and Open Source:} All third-party libraries (FastAPI, XGBoost, scikit-learn, Adafruit driver libraries) are released under permissive open-source licenses (MIT, BSD, Apache 2.0). The complete project repository is licensed under the permissive MIT License.
    \item \textbf{Data Privacy:} The platform captures strictly environmental physics telemetry (temperature, humidity, soil moisture, barometric pressure). No personal identifying information (PII), visual camera streams, or biometric records are gathered, ensuring compliance with Nepal's \emph{Individual Privacy Act, 2075 (2018)}.
    \item \textbf{Electrical Safety:} Edge hardware operates on low-voltage direct current ($3.3\,\text{V}$ logic, $5\,\text{V}$ microcontroller supply, and $12\,\text{V}$ pump). Optocoupled isolation on the relay physically isolates low-voltage microelectronics from the pump power supply.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Operational Feasibility}
The system was engineered for continuous reliability in rural and peri-urban Nepalese agricultural environments:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Farmer Usability:} To overcome digital literacy barriers, the web interface translates complex physical indices into plain-language advice (\emph{``Water the plants now''} vs. \emph{``Soil is fine — keep the pump off''}) alongside color-coded status badges, eliminating the need to interpret raw sensor charts.
    \item \textbf{Sensor Durability and Maintenance:} The capacitive probe v1.2 utilizes insulated dielectric sensing, eliminating the rapid electrolytic corrosion seen in cheap resistive probes. Maintenance is limited to periodic surface wiping and pump intake mesh cleaning.
    \item \textbf{Power Resilience:} Low energy draw allows the system to be readily backed up with an affordable $12\,\text{V}$ lead-acid battery or a small $20\,\text{W}$ solar panel to survive load shedding and rural grid brownouts.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Social and Environmental Feasibility}
The project aligns with community dynamics, labor division, and environmental preservation in Nepal:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Technology Adoption and Social Acceptability:} Nepalese greenhouse growers in peri-urban corridors (Kathmandu Valley, Bhaktapur, Kavrepalanchok) already invest in polyhouse plastic tunnels and drip manifolds; adding an automated edge controller seamlessly integrates into existing practices.
    \item \textbf{Community Water Equity:} In rural irrigation cooperatives sharing communal boreholes or seasonal storage tanks, reducing individual tomato tunnel water usage by over $40\%$ alleviates aquifer depletion and mitigates water-sharing conflicts.
    \item \textbf{Labor Quality of Life:} Automating pump toggling relieves household members (often women and elders who manage greenhouse maintenance) from the demanding routine of manual valve switching twice daily, freeing up labor for pruning, harvesting, and market distribution without displacing agricultural employment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ethical, Societal, and Environmental Governance}
\label{app:ethics}

\textbf{Farmer Data Privacy and Sovereignty:} The system collects strictly physical environmental measurements (temperature, moisture, pressure, pump state). No personal identifiers, camera feeds, or geolocation traces are harvested. All telemetry resides on the local farm compute host, safeguarding smallholder sovereignty against data commercialization.

\textbf{Environmental Sustainability:} By curtailing pump run-time by over 41\%, the system conserves scarce groundwater and diminishes electrical energy consumption. Preventing soil waterlogging avoids anaerobic soil conditions and suppresses fungal outbreaks without synthetic agrochemicals.

\textbf{Academic and Technical Integrity:} All design revisions—including the intentional pivot from LSTM to XGBoost and candid disclosure of soil probe calibration realities—are transparently documented to uphold scientific integrity and provide an honest reference architecture for future agricultural engineering students.
